How Educational Institutions Can Use AI

What AI looks like in practice for administrators, admissions teams, teachers and students, and the privacy rules every school has to respect.

In this article

  1. Strategic Applications Across Educational Personas
  2. Words of Caution: Safeguarding Privacy and Maintaining Accuracy

The integration of artificial intelligence into primary, secondary, and higher education is often framed around extremes. On one side is the fear of automated plagiarism while the other is the hype of fully autonomous classrooms.

The actual reality of AI in education is far more practical. Artificial intelligence acts as a force multiplier for school operations when it properly sits in the middle of complex edtech systems. It bridges the gap between massive institutional data pools and the personal, human-driven work of teaching and learning.

Understanding how AI can be used in schools requires looking past generic chat tools to examine how specific stakeholders such as administrators, admissions teams, educators, and students can leverage custom AI systems to improve outcomes.

Strategic Applications Across Educational Personas

  1. 01AdministratorsBudgets, facilities and staffing data
  2. 02AdmissionsFaster, fairer applicant review
  3. 03EducatorsEarly warning on struggling students
  4. 04StudentsA learning partner, not a ghostwriter

1. Administrators: Synthesizing Institutional Data Sets

School districts, colleges, and university systems generate millions of data points every term that span budgetary allocations, facility utilization, staffing ratios, and district-wide academic performance metrics. Synthesizing this data typically requires weeks of manual reporting across siloed databases when AI is not in the picture.

Custom administrative AI engines act as intelligent search and analytical layers over institutional repositories. District leaders and campus department heads can query complex data systems using conversational prompts:

“Compare our Q3 facility maintenance expenditures across all high school campuses against our allocated operational budgets for 2025 and highlight anomalies above 15%.”

Instead of waiting for an external audit or manual spreadsheet consolidation, administrative teams receive aggregated, verified reports in real time, allowing leadership to make data-backed operational decisions quickly. AI-integrated Student Information Systems can allow administrators to quickly synthesize data on students as well.

2. Enrollment & Admissions Officers: Streamlining Applicant Evaluation

Admissions departments, particularly those in post-secondary education, face seasonal surges of incoming applications, transcripts, recommendation letters, and financial aid forms. Evaluating these files manually creates significant processing backlogs and delays communication with prospective students.

AI-driven enrollment software streamlines the top-of-funnel admissions workflow without sacrificing thorough evaluation:

  • Document Parsing & Extraction: Automatically extract GPA metrics, course credits, standardized test scores, and transfer requirements from transcripts across varied formats.
  • Applicant Matching: Aggregate student interest profiles with available scholarship criteria, institutional program capacities, and housing preferences.
  • Predictive Yield Modeling: Analyze historical enrollment trends to help universities predict matriculation rates and optimize financial aid allocation strategies.

By automating manual document verification, enrollment officers can devote more time to high-value student engagement and direct interview evaluations.

3. Educators: Early Intervention & Data-Driven Student Support

One of the greatest challenges teachers face is identifying struggling students before they fail a major exam or drop out of a course. When a single teacher oversees dozens or hundreds of students, subtle drops in homework completion or attendance often go unnoticed until it is too late.

Integrated Learning Management Systems serve as an early-warning support system for teachers. By continuously aggregating attendance logs, pop quiz performance, and assignment submission times the platform can identify negative trajectory patterns early.

An educator receives an automated dashboard flag: Student A has missed three consecutive homework submissions and scored 20% below their historical baseline on recent quizzes. Equipped with this insight, the teacher can step in with targeted academic support, personalized review materials, or a 1-on-1 check-in before the student falls further behind.

4. Students: AI as an Interactive Learning Partner, Not a Content Generator

For students, the value of AI in the classroom depends heavily on how the technology is framed. AI hinders critical thinking and skill development when used merely to write essays and solve math problems. However, AI transforms into a 24/7 accessible learning partner when deployed properly.

The operational distinction comes down to guided iteration versus execution:

Misaligned usage: generating work

“Write an introductory paragraph for my essay on American history.”

Result: The student bypasses the learning process entirely.

Constructive usage: guided learning

“Here is the introductory paragraph I wrote for my essay. Please evaluate my thesis statement and provide feedback on how I can improve the transition to my supporting arguments.”

Result: The student retains full authorship while gaining immediate structural feedback.

Custom educational software can establish guardrails that limit direct content generation, prompting students with diagnostic questions and structural feedback instead of handing them completed answers.

Words of Caution: Safeguarding Privacy and Maintaining Accuracy

Deploying artificial intelligence within educational institutions introduces regulatory, legal, and operational considerations that require strict governance.

1. FERPA and COPPA Compliance Are Essential

Student data privacy is protected by strict federal regulations. The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) prohibits disclosing personally identifiable information (PII) from education records without consent, while the Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) regulates data collection for children under 13.

FERPA
Education records
No sharing of personally identifiable information from student records without consent.
COPPA
Children under 13
Strict limits on collecting personal data from young children online.

Uploading student transcripts, behavioral notes, grades, or written essays into public, consumer-facing AI platforms such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini violates federal privacy frameworks. Public platforms frequently store input data to train future foundational models, exposing private institutional records. It is possible to build “closed loop” AI systems where educational institutions can use the latest AI models but not have their data sent back outside for models to train on. Additionally, it is possible to build private models that are trained on a school’s unique documents so that an AI can produce documents in a similar format.

Did you know?

FERPA became law in 1974, decades before AI tools existed. Its rules still apply to every system that touches student records, including the AI ones.

2. Controlling Algorithmic Hallucinations

AI models operate probabilistically by predicting word sequences rather than understanding factual truth. When queried about specialized academic subjects or grading standards, ungrounded AI models can fabricate inaccurate historical facts, miscalculate numerical data, or invent citations. These misguided outputs are often referred to as “hallucinations” since they are neither grounded in reality nor facts.

Schools must enforce a strict “human-in-the-loop” mandate. AI should never be permitted to automatically assign final grades, issue disciplinary actions, or evaluate admissions files without direct review and sign-off by a qualified educator or administrator.

Always signed off by a person

  • Final grades
  • Disciplinary actions
  • Admissions decisions

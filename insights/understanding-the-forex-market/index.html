Understanding the Forex Market: Everything You Need to Know About Foreign Exchange

In this article

  1. What Is Forex Trading?
  2. How Currency Trading Works
  3. Key Terminology and Market Mechanics
  4. Macroeconomic Drivers of Currency Values
  5. Essential Risk Management Best Practices
  6. Navigating Modern Financial Markets

The Foreign Exchange market, commonly known as “forex” or “FX”, is the world’s largest and most liquid financial market. With a staggering average daily trading volume exceeding $9 trillion, forex operates globally across major financial hubs facilitating international trade, cross-border investments, and speculative trading.

Grasping how currency trading works provides a foundational look into modern international finance for trading desks looking to diversify their portfolio or for those looking to understand the macroeconomic forces that shape global commerce.

$9T+

changes hands on the forex market on an average day, more than any other financial market in the world.

Bank for International Settlements, Triennial Survey 2025

What Is Forex Trading?

At its core, foreign exchange is the process of converting one national currency into another. Unlike traditional equity markets that operate through a centralized exchange floor, forex is an over-the-counter (OTC) market. Transactions take place electronically across a decentralized, global network of central banks, commercial financial institutions, institutional brokers, and retail traders.

The forex market remains active 24 hours a day, five days a week because global economies operate across staggered time zones. Trading continuously cycles through major global financial hubs starting in Sydney and Tokyo before moving through London and closing in New York.

How Currency Trading Works

Currencies are always traded in pairs because every transaction involves simultaneously purchasing one currency while selling another. In any currency pair, the first currency is designated as the base currency, while the second is the quote currency (or counter currency).

For example, if the EUR/USD pair is trading at 1.0850, the Euro is the base currency and the U.S. Dollar is the quote currency. This price means that 1 Euro is worth $1.0850 USD. If a trader anticipates that the Euro will strengthen relative to the Dollar, they execute a buy position (going long). Conversely, if they expect the Euro to lose value against the Dollar, they execute a sell position (going short).

EUR/USD1.0850

  • Base currencyThe one you are buying or selling: 1 Euro.
  • Quote currencyWhat it costs in the second currency: $1.0850.
  • Buy (long)You expect the Euro to strengthen against the Dollar.
  • Sell (short)You expect the Euro to weaken against the Dollar.

Market instruments are generally grouped into three main categories based on volume and region:

  • Major Pairs

    The most heavily traded combinations feature the U.S. Dollar paired with major global economies such as EUR/USD, GBP/USD, and USD/JPY. These pairs offer the highest market liquidity and the tightest transaction spreads.

  • Minor Pairs (Crosses)

    Transactions between major currencies that exclude the U.S. Dollar entirely such as EUR/GBP or AUD/JPY.

  • Exotic Pairs

    A major currency paired with the currency of a developing or emerging market economy such as USD/TRY (U.S. Dollar / Turkish Lira) or EUR/SEK (Euro / Swedish Krona).

Key Terminology and Market Mechanics

Navigating currency markets requires familiarity with several fundamental financial concepts that dictate trade execution and pricing structure.

Pip
1.0850 → 1.0851
One pip is 0.0001 on most pairs. This move is +1 pip.
Spread
Ask − Bid
The gap between the buying and selling price, and the main cost of a trade.
Lot
100,000 units
One standard lot. Mini is 10,000 and micro is 1,000 units.

Price movements in forex are measured in pips (Percentage in Point) which represent the standardized unit of change in an exchange rate. This is typically the fourth decimal place (0.0001) for most pairs. The cost of executing a trade is primarily reflected in the spread, which is the difference between the bid (buying) price and the ask (selling) price quoted by a liquidity provider or broker.

Trade sizes are standardized into lots. A standard lot represents 100,000 units of the base currency, though financial platforms frequently offer mini (10,000 units) and micro (1,000 units) lots to accommodate varying capital sizes.

Institutions and traders utilize leverage which allows a market participant to hold a large position by putting up a smaller upfront cash deposit, known as margin, to manage larger contract sizes. While leverage amplifies capital efficiency and potential returns, it proportionately magnifies downside financial risk.

Key term

Margin: the deposit that opens a leveraged position. At 20:1 leverage, $5,000 of margin controls one standard lot ($100,000). A 1% move in the rate is then a $1,000 gain or loss, or 20% of the deposit.

Macroeconomic Drivers of Currency Values

Currency exchange rates fluctuate continuously based on shifting supply and demand dynamics across international borders.

Central bank monetary policy serves as the primary driver of exchange rate direction. Decisions on benchmark interest rates by institutions like the Federal Reserve or the European Central Bank heavily influence currency demand, as higher interest rates typically attract foreign capital seeking higher yields.

Additionally, periodic economic data releases including Gross Domestic Product (GDP), Consumer Price Index (CPI) inflation metrics, employment reports, and trade balances constantly shift market sentiment. Broader geopolitical developments, such as election cycles, international trade agreements, and fiscal policy changes, also play a decisive role in shaping long-term currency valuations.

Essential Risk Management Best Practices

Given the inherent volatility of global currency markets, disciplined risk management is critical to capital preservation.

Professional trading desks and risk managers rely on automated stop-loss orders to automatically close out positions if prices move against a trade beyond a predetermined threshold. Capital exposure per transaction is strictly controlled, with best practices dictating that traders limit total risk to a small, fixed percentage of overall portfolio value on any single position.

Did you know?

A widely used rule of thumb caps the risk on any single position at around 1–2% of the portfolio. Even ten losing trades in a row would then leave most of the capital intact.

Finally, analyzing market mechanics within simulated demo environments allows teams to backtest strategies, evaluate liquidity conditions, and verify platform execution protocols before deploying live institutional capital.

Navigating Modern Financial Markets

Understanding the mechanics of foreign exchange provides essential context for analyzing international trade, corporate cash flows, and global economic health. Whether analyzing macro trends or evaluating active execution strategies, long-term success in currency markets depends on structured analysis, rigorous risk protocols, and disciplined market observation.
